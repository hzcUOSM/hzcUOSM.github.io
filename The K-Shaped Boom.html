The K-Shaped Boom
By Matt Schleys Originally published in The Japan Times

Much has been written about the “K-shaped economy,” in which the rich get richer and the poor get poorer. Japanese has an even better character to represent this trend: く (ku).


For both workers and studios alike, the anime industry is looking increasingly く-shaped. Earlier this year, Aniplex reported record sales for fiscal 2025, while two of its subsidiary studios, CloverWorks and Boundary, posted net losses. According to research firm Teikoku Databank, in fiscal 2025, 45.7% of all anime companies showed increased profits, while 53.5% reported either a decrease in profits or losses compared with the previous year.

Pressures on studios include increased costs for labor but also for materials such as data servers and tablet computers, whose prices have been impacted by the worldwide chip shortage and the weak yen.


“(Digitalization) has become one of the biggest costs for anime studios right now, especially for the small ones, which may not have enough profit margin to be able to invest in all of those capital expenses,” says Richardson Handjaja, publisher and editor of the Animenomics newsletter. “But if they don't, they get left behind.”


“Skilled workers tend to go to places with better environments, so smaller places have a harder time keeping talent, and that means that their work gets delayed,” says Yuga Tokuno, a JAniCA board member and animator. “If you have delays, then you naturally have to pay people more, and that makes the difference between the two environments ever more severe.”

In the 2026 survey, 44.9% of respondents were seishain (permanent employees), compared to just 15.5% in 2015. “Given the current situation, the reality is that unless you make full-time employment a condition for hiring, you simply won't attract top talent,” says Tokuno. “One large company that used to get about 200 applications (per year) recently only had about 40 because they don't offer full-time employment, while even smaller, lesser-known companies that offer full-time are getting more applicants.”


Meanwhile, young animators entering the industry who are lucky or skilled enough to land jobs at well-resourced studios reap the benefits of full-time contracts and good working environments. But that is not the case for everyone.

“There's definitely two sides of the coin,” says Handjaja. “I think one reason for that disparity is the inconsistent training environment in the industry right now, where you have certain studios that train their animators very well, and then you have studios that don't.”


Some companies, like Bandai Namco and P.A. Works, have launched in-house academies taught by current professionals, while more studios now offer training programs to new recruits. One way the industry is attempting to improve margins and efficiency is through consolidation. MAPPA recently absorbed sister studio Contrail, while Kadokawa has announced it will consolidate its multiple studios into a single facility.
